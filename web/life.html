ibn ʿAbd al-Salām ibn Taymiyya al-Ḥarrānī

Sixty-five years, from a town the Mongols emptied to a cell in the citadel of Damascus. Nine summonses to trial. Six imprisonments. Two exiles. Five hundred thousand at his funeral. This is the road he walked, station by station.

٦٥٦ هـ · 656 AH · 1258 CE

The world that received him

Five years before he was born, the Tatars came down on Baghdad. The library went into the river until the water ran black. The Caliph was rolled in a carpet and trampled. Five centuries of the ʿAbbāsid house ended in a week, and the Ummah woke without a head.

What was left was pieces. Egypt held under the Baḥrī Mamlūks, who kept a shadow-caliph in Cairo for the look of the thing. Greater Syria was carved among petty amīrs too busy with each other to face an army. On the coast the Franks still held ʿAkkā. In Tabrīz sat the Īlkhāns, sons of the men who had burned Baghdad. And in every quarter, ordinary Muslims lived with the knowledge that the horizon could produce horsemen at any hour.

Monday, 10 Rabīʿ al-Awwal 661 · 22 January 1263

Ḥarrān

He was born into a house that had already given the Ḥanbalīs two of their imāms. His grandfather Majd al-Dīn ibn Taymiyya, author of al-Muntaqā, had died six years before him. The family remembered him as a man who could hold ḥadīth and men's schools of thought in his head at once. His uncle Fakhr al-Dīn had been an authority before that. His father ʿAbd al-Ḥalīm was a muḥaddith who would later preach from the minbar of the Umayyad Mosque.

So the child had a name before he had a memory. What he did not have was safety. The Mongol frontier lay a few days' ride away, and it was moving.

٦٦٧ هـ · 667 AH · 1269 CE · he is six

The night they left

The family fled Ḥarrān before the westward advance of the Mongols and did not stop until Damascus, some four hundred miles of road with children and a household of books. It is said they had no beast for the load and drew the cart of the library themselves.

He was six years old. He would never see his birthplace again, and forty years later, when a Mongol sultan offered to make him its governor, he refused it without a pause. What the horsemen planted in the boy was not fear. It was a lifelong intolerance of the man who breaks a covenant.

The savagery of those invaders had undoubtedly given the boy a deep hatred of oppression.
دمشق · The Ḥanbalī quarter

A city that took them in

They settled in the Ḥanbalī quarter, in the shadow of the Ḥanbalī gate outside the walls of the old city. His father was given the direction of the Sukkariyya madrasa and a chair for ḥadīth in the Umayyad Mosque and in Dār al-Ḥadīth al-ʿAsakariyya. They came in as refugees. Within a season they were teaching.

This is the world the boy grew inside. The call to prayer off the Umayyad minarets before dawn, the booksellers' lane, the reading circles that ran from ʿaṣr into the night, and old men in the courtyard who had heard ḥadīth from men who had heard it from men, an unbroken chain reaching back six hundred years to Madīna.

الحياة اليومية · How people lived

The world outside his door

To understand what he gave up, you have to see what there was. Damascus in his boyhood was one of the richest cities on earth, a walled town of some hundred thousand souls inside seven gates, wrapped in the Ghūṭa, an oasis of apricot, walnut and mulberry orchards fed by the Baradā, which the city split above its walls into channels and ran under its own streets.

The family did not live in the grand centre. They were refugees in the Ḥanbalī quarter, the same slope of Mount Qāsiyūn, al-Ṣāliḥiyya, that an earlier generation of Ḥanbalī exiles from Jerusalem had settled when the Franks took their city. A suburb of displaced people, thick with madrasas, that produced more scholars than the capital below it.

Before dawn
The gates are still shut. The muʾadhdhins go up, and the bread ovens are already lit, because most houses have no oven and the quarter shares one.
The quarter
A ḥāra is a world of its own. Its own lane, its own gate closed at night, its own mosque, its own headman. You are known by it, married within it, buried from it.
The house
Blank wall to the street. Everything faces an inner courtyard with a basin and a citrus tree. Cushions, mats, a chest, a brazier. Furniture as we mean it does not exist.
The market
Sūqs sorted by trade, grain, cloth, sword-smiths, the booksellers hard against the Umayyad Mosque. The muḥtasib walks them checking weights, bread and honest dealing.
The bath
Dozens of ḥammāms, heated on the city's own water, open to men by day and women by afternoon. For most people the only hot water there is.
School
A boy goes to the kuttāb at five or six to learn the Qurʾān on a wooden tablet, washing it clean each time he has the passage by heart.
Learning is free
Madrasas are endowed by waqf, a rich man's property tied up forever to pay a teacher and feed students. A porter's son can sit in the same circle as a judge's, and did.
And death is near
Famine follows a bad harvest within the year. Fever empties a lane in a season. An army can appear on the eastern road at any time. Everyone here has buried children.
His boyhood · نشأته

Forty dirhams, refused

His father wanted him encouraged. He went to the man teaching the boy Qurʾān and put forty dirhams in his hand and said, give him these every month, he is young, it may gladden him and increase his zeal. The child would not take them. O teacher, he said, I promised Allāh that I would not take payment for the Qurʾān. The teacher said afterwards that this comes from no boy except one whom Allāh is protecting.

He memorised the Qurʾān as a youth, then ḥadīth, then fiqh, then the Arabic language, until he excelled in all of them. The Musnad of Aḥmad, the two Ṣaḥīḥs, Tirmidhī, Abū Dāwūd, al-Nasāʾī, Ibn Mājah, al-Dāraquṭnī, each was recited to him from beginning to end, more than once. The first ḥadīth book he committed to memory was al-Ḥumaydī's al-Jamʿ bayn al-Ṣaḥīḥayn. Those who watched him said it was as though knowledge had been mixed into his flesh and his blood.

On the road to the library

A Jew lived on the road the boy took to the library, and would stop him with questions, pressing him, fascinated by how quickly the answers came. In time the boy began handing him, each meeting, one more thing that showed him the falsehood he was upon. It went on until the man entered Islām and became a practising Muslim.

He sat with two hundred, and belonged to none of them

Al-Dhahabī preserved a list in his own hand, forty-one men and four women. Others counted past two hundred. Yet no one of them shaped him enough for him to be called anyone's disciple, a thing his admirers thought a wonder and his enemies thought a fault.

He read past his teachers as well, into philosophy, kalām, the heresiographies of al-Ashʿarī and al-Shahrastānī, the Sufis, al-Junayd, Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī (whose Qūt al-qulūb was among his favourite books), al-Qushayrī, al-Ghazālī, al-Anṣārī al-Harawī, ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī, on whose Futūḥ al-ghayb he wrote a commentary. He called several of them our shaykh. He would still spend his life refuting what he believed they had got wrong.

طلب العلم · The mechanics of a life

How knowledge was actually taken

Not from books. From a person, in a room, with witnesses, and the proof of it written on the manuscript afterwards.

01
الحَلْقة · The circle

The shaykh sits with his back to a pillar of the mosque, and the students sit around him on the floor in a ring, the nearest ones the closest to him. There is no enrolment and no fee. You come, or you do not.

02
السَّماع · Hearing

A reader recites the book aloud to the shaykh while everyone follows. Mishear a name in a chain and you are corrected on the spot, as one reader was, racing through an isnād, by Ibn Taymiyya, who was right.

03
الطِّباق · The audition note

At the end, a certificate is written onto the manuscript itself, giving the title, the date, the place, and the name of every single person in the room, men, women, servants, and the babies carried in. Those notes are how we still know who was there.

04
الإجازة · The licence

A shaykh grants you permission to transmit what you heard from him. Families collected them for infants so the child would one day hold the shortest chain alive, Zaynab bint al-Kamāl had her first at one year old.

05
الرِّحلة · The journey

If the last man alive with a high chain to al-Bukhārī is in Baghdad, you go to Baghdad. Men crossed deserts for one sitting. It is why the death of an old woman in Damascus could be news in Cairo.

06
الحفظ · Carrying it in the head

A manuscript costs what a craftsman earns in weeks and there is one copy of it. So the book that matters is the one inside you, which is exactly why a cell with no library could not stop him writing.

Al-Bazzār came to Damascus to read Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and meant to do it privately, out of modesty. When Ibn Taymiyya heard, he made him read it in front of a large gathering of men, women and children, this should never be done except in a way that benefits the Muslims, and sat beside him for all twenty sessions with his own copy, checking the reader against a manuscript more accurate than the one being read.

Twenty sessions, every day but Friday
٦٨٣-٦٨٤ هـ · 1284-1285 CE · he is twenty-one

His father's chair

ʿAbd al-Ḥalīm died in 682. The following year the son took his place as director of the Sukkariyya, twenty-one years old, in a seat his father, his grandfather and his uncle had filled before him.

His first public lesson was given there at twenty-two. Ibn Kathīr writes that the room was crowded, that Shaykh Tāj al-Dīn al-Fizārī wrote the whole session down for the benefit in it, and that the audience thanked him profusely, astonished at his age. On the tenth of Ṣafar 684 he mounted a pulpit built for him in the Umayyad Mosque and began the Qurʾān from its opening. He would still be at it, year after year, for the rest of his life in the city.

Just as Allāh made iron soft for Dāwūd, He made the sciences supple in Ibn Taymiyya's grasp.

An eyewitness · al-Bazzār, who never missed a lesson

He prepared nothing. He would sit after praying two rakʿas, praise Allāh, and send blessings upon the Messenger in a manner so sweet I never heard the like of it, and then he would speak.

Allāh would open upon him knowledge, gems, hidden benefits, narrations, evidences drawn out of verses and ḥadīth, the sayings of the scholars weighed one against another, and old Arabic poetry brought in to carry the point, sometimes with the poet's name.

His speech was as smooth as a flowing river, and he moved as the ocean moves. From the beginning of it to the end he seemed to be in another world, his eyes closed. He did not do it for effect. It was something given. He would stay like that until he was finished, and it would shake our hearts.

Then he would open his eyes and go out among the people with a bright and cheerful face, as if meeting them for the first time, and sometimes apologise to one of them for what he thought had been a shortcoming in his talk.

Preaching in the Umayyad Mosque was a public office. What was said from that pulpit reached the governor by evening, and half the city could quote it by the next Friday.

عبادته · A day in his life

From one night to the next

Al-Bazzār lodged with him and wrote down what he saw. Nothing here is legend. It is the ordinary shape of his days, kept up until he died.

By Allāh, until this day there has never been a time in my life more beloved to me than the time I spent with him. Subḥān Allāh, how short those days were. If only they had been longer.
The night
Alone. He would cut himself off from everyone, secluded with his Lord, holding to his recitation and to the litanies of the night.
Fajr
When he said the opening takbīr your heart wanted to fly out of its place. His limbs shook. He lengthened the recitation as the Prophet ﷺ used to lengthen it.
Until sunrise
Dhikr, and al-Fātiḥa repeated over and over, his eyes turned up to the sky as though something there had caught them and would not let go.
Forenoon
Ḍuḥā, then off at speed to wherever ḥadīth was being read. If he saw an evil in the street he stopped and removed it. If he heard of a funeral he ran to pray it.
Midday
Back to his mosque, giving fatwā and settling people's needs until Ẓuhr. His circle was open to all, old and young, rich and poor, free and slave, men and women.
Evening
Maghrib and voluntary prayer, then his own writings read back to him, and he would add. Then ʿIshāʾ, then knowledge again until much of the night had gone.
Every week
The hospital. He visited the sick, the poor ones, the strangers with no one, and prayed over them.
زهده · An inventory

Everything he owned

01The clothes he had on. When a poor man called to him in the street and he had nothing, he took off part of what he was wearing and gave it, saying, this is all I have, and I am sorry I have nothing more.
02Half a turban. A man came needing something to wrap his head, and without being asked, he cut his own turban in two, wound one half around his head and handed over the other.
03Bread, about half a raṭl for the day, broken with whoever was there. He would lift his hand from it early so his guest would eat, and would not move the piece until the man was full.
04Loaves hidden in his sleeve, to be pressed on some poor man on the way to a ḥadīth session when he thought his students were not looking.
05Books, which he gave away. A man asked for one and was told to go into the library and choose. He took an expensive Muṣḥaf. Those present objected. He said, Is it right that I withhold from him what he asked for? Leave him. He may benefit.
06Nothing else. He never traded, never took a gift from a ruler or a governor or a merchant, was never put in charge of anyone's money, and never saved a dirham. The only thing he produced in his life, and the only thing he left behind to be inherited, was knowledge.
What he looked like

Al-Dhahabī sat with him and described him. His skin was white, his hair and beard dark, his moustache light. His hair came down to the bottoms of his ears. His eyes, he said, were like two speaking tongues. He was of middle height with wide shoulders, a strong voice and an eloquent tongue, reading at first quickly and hard, and ending soft.

He wore whatever was there and ate whatever was put down. He never dressed for a visitor, never asked for a particular cloth, and would keep one set on so long it went dirty without his noticing. It was his family who asked whether it wanted washing.

A madrasa professorship carried a salary, bread, and often a house. He held two such posts and lived as though he held none.

When they left the house to read, he carried the books himself and would let no one else carry them. Told this was not fitting, he answered, If I carried it on my head, my head would not deserve to carry something that contains the words of the Messenger ﷺ.

المماليك · The men who ruled him

Slaves who became sultans

Every man who signed an order against Ibn Taymiyya had been bought as a boy. The Mamlūks were children taken from the Qipchaq steppe and the Caucasus, sold into the barracks of the Cairo citadel, taught Islām and Arabic and the bow and the sabre, then freed, and bound for life not to a dynasty but to the household that had raised them.

It made them the finest army in the world. They had stopped the Mongols at ʿAyn Jālūt when nobody else could, and they would push the last Franks into the sea. It also meant power passed by faction and not by blood. A sultan reigned as long as the amīrs behind him held together, and no longer. That is why a scholar in Damascus could be honoured by one ruler and imprisoned by the next inside a single year.

And it is why his enemies never needed to defeat his arguments. They only needed to reach whoever was holding the seal that month.

Civilisation is rooted in justice, and the consequences of oppression are devastating. Therefore it is said that Allāh aids the just state even if it is not Muslim, and withholds His help from the oppressive state even if it is Muslim.
Ibn Taymiyya, on government

He had no patience either for the pious retreat from office. Some Muslims, he wrote, think that taking a high post inevitably brings love of rule and wealth, and that a man who accepts one has turned away from the religion of mercy and humility. He disagreed, and wrote that the appointment of the virtuous serves the Ummah far better than assigning posts to the wicked.

٦٩٠ هـ · 690 AH · 1291 CE · he is twenty-eight

ʿAkkā

Scholars of every school went out to the siege of ʿAkkā, the last crusader city on the coast. He went with them. Those who were there said they saw him show a bravery that could not properly be described, and that his counsel and his eye for the ground were part of why the city was taken.

He was a scholar who could ride. On a horse he handled it with precision and went into the middle of the enemy, sitting like the firmest of knights, calling a takbīr that did the enemy more damage than a charge.

٦٩٢ هـ · 692 AH · 1293 CE · he is thirty-one

The road to Makka

The Syrian caravan left Damascus in Shawwāl, as it did every year, with thousands of pilgrims, a military escort, water carried for the dry stages, and a road south through Bosrā, Maʿān and Tabūk that had been walked for six centuries. He went with it.

It was the one journey in his life that nobody forced on him, and the only time he stood outside the politics of Cairo and Damascus entirely. He wrote a manual of the rites, a Mansak, and questions put to him on ḥajj fill a long stretch of the fatāwā, on what the pilgrim in iḥrām may and may not do, how a woman travels, what to do when the caravan is late for Ẓuhr.

He also saw, gathered in one place, what pilgrims from every corner of the Ummah actually did, the sacrifices left at tombs along the road, the calling on the dead, the practices no scholar in a Damascus lecture hall would have described to him. Much of what he spent the last twenty years of his life writing about, and finally went to prison for, he first saw on this journey.

٦٩٣ هـ · 693 AH · 1294 CE

The first blood was his own

A Christian named ʿAssāf was said to have insulted the Prophet ﷺ in public. Ibn Taymiyya and another shaykh took the matter to the viceroy, who summoned the man. A crowd gathered, tempers went, and it was the two shaykhs, not the accused, whom the viceroy had flogged and briefly held.

He was thirty-one. It was his first taste of a prison and the first time he learned what it costs to be right in front of power. Out of that single incident came al-Ṣārim al-Maslūl ʿalā shātim al-Rasūl, the drawn sword against the one who insults the Messenger.

العدل · The scholar in the street

Bread, and what justice meant

It is easy to read his life as a quarrel between scholars. Most of it was not. He lived among people who could be ruined by one dry winter, and a great deal of what he said was about them.

When grain ran short, the men who held it waited for the price to climb. He went after them directly, and he was willing to say the thing that jurists are usually careful about, that the ruler may compel a hoarder to sell.

In times of need, the ruler can force people to sell their goods at their original value.

He did the small version of it himself, every week. He visited the hospital, the sick poor, the strangers with nobody. He put money into the hand of a man newly arrived in the city who knew no one and had begun to walk the streets like someone lost. He told a young man he had prayed over to go home to the wife and four daughters he had left without a provider, and the man wept, because nobody in Damascus knew that about him.

What a hard year looked like
The harvest fails. The Ghūṭa feeds the city, and when it is short there is no second source, because grain travels badly and the roads are not safe.
The price of bread doubles. Day-labourers eat bread and little else. A doubling is not inconvenience. It is hunger by the end of the month.
The countryside comes in. Villagers arrive at the gates. The quarters take in what they can, and the rest sleep in the mosque courtyards.
Then fever. Crowding and hunger together, and a lane can lose its children in a season. This is the ordinary background of every year in this story.
And the waqf holds. The endowments keep paying for the hospital, the soup, and the madrasa's bread. A city's charity was built to outlive the men who founded it.

He took nothing from any of it. Not a gift from a ruler, a governor or a merchant, never a partnership, never a piece of land. He was offered the chief judgeship of the realm and refused it, and he died owning his clothes.

٦٩٨ هـ · 698 AH · 1299 CE

A question from Ḥamā

He answered it with al-Fatwā al-Ḥamawiyya al-kubrā, a statement of creed hostile to Ashʿarī theology and to kalām as a method. His opponents read it as anthropomorphism and had him summoned to the house of the Shāfiʿī judge Jalāl al-Dīn al-Qazwīnī.

The text was read closely, he explained it line by line, and he was acquitted of every charge and left free to teach and to write. It was the first of nine such summonses. He would win most of them, and be imprisoned all the same.

When the deputy sultan tried to smooth it over by announcing that these were simply the positions of Imām Aḥmad, he refused the peace offering. It was the creed of the Salaf, he said, and not the private property of Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal.

٦٩٩ هـ · 699 AH · 1300 CE

Knees almost touching

The Mongols were in Damascus. The city's people were terrified and some meant to run. He would not hear of it. When the generals said say inshāʾAllāh, he answered, I say it in certainty, not in hope. Then he walked out with a delegation of the city's leaders to sit in front of the Īlkhān Ghāzān.

Ghāzān had taken a great sum from the king of the Georgians in exchange for a free hand against the Muslims of Damascus. That was the first thing Ibn Taymiyya raised. Shaykh Wajīh al-Dīn ibn al-Munajjā, who was in the room, said he kept reminding the sultan of the words of Allāh and His Messenger about justice, raising his voice, edging nearer as he spoke until his knees were almost against the sultan's, and the sultan, on his knees, did not once look away.

He left that tent with the blood of the city spared, its women and children protected, and most of the Muslim captives released back to their families.

You claim to be a Muslim, that you have qāḍīs and scholars and men who call the adhān, and still you invaded us. Your father and your grandfather were disbelievers, and they never did what you have done. We had a pact and we kept it. You gave a promise, and you broke your promise.

Ghāzān asked who this man was. I have never seen anyone like him, he said, nor a heart as firm as his, nor have any words moved me as his have. Then he offered him Ḥarrān, the land of his fathers, to govern.

By Allāh, I will never leave the land that Ibrāhīm migrated to, and take another home in exchange for it.

2 Ramaḍān 702 · 20 April 1303

Shaqḥab

A third invasion was coming. The people of Shām sent him to Cairo to move the sultan, and when al-Nāṣir Muḥammad hesitated, he told him plainly, if you turn your back on Syria, we will appoint over it a sultan who can defend it and enjoy it in peace. The army marched.

They met at Shaqḥab, south of Damascus, in Ramaḍān. He gave the army the fatwā to break the fast so they would meet the enemy strong, as the Prophet ﷺ had commanded near Makka, and then he went along the ranks himself, among the soldiers and the commanders, eating from the food in his hand so that they would see it and do it.

Where he saw men going soft he steadied them. He raised their spirits, reminded them of what Allāh has for the ones who go out, and promised them victory. When the three days had passed, the thing he had promised them in the city was in the middle of Damascus for everyone to see.

٧٠٥ هـ · 705 AH · 1306 CE

Acquitted, and banished anyway

Two councils met back to back in the governor's residence over al-ʿAqīda al-Wāsiṭiyya. In the second, a student of al-Rāzī, the master of late Ashʿarī kalām, pronounced the creed in conformity with the Qurʾān and the Sunna. The Shāfiʿī judge Najm al-Dīn ibn Ṣaṣrā reopened the case at once. A third council was called by order of the sultan. It, too, declined to condemn him. Ibn Ṣaṣrā resigned, and a few months later both men were banished to Cairo.

In Damascus he was too loved to be touched. In Cairo he was a stranger. His enemies understood this perfectly.

The people gathered to bid him farewell, overwhelmed with grief and astonishment, many of them weeping.Ibn ʿAbd al-Hādī, his student, on the day he left
٧٠٥-٧٠٧ هـ · Eighteen months

The citadel of Cairo

He arrived and was put straight in front of another council, Mamlūk officers and the four chief judges of Egypt. He felt that his arbiter was also his adversary and refused to answer. They convicted him of anthropomorphism and sent him to the citadel.

They took his books away. So he wrote from memory, works small and large, packed with ḥadīth, reports of the Companions, names of transmitters and authors and their titles, each attributed to its source, and the volume in which it stood, and where in the volume. When these were later checked against the books, not one mistake was found in them, and nothing needed changing.

He was released in 707 after the intervention of an Arab prince, Ḥusām al-Dīn ibn ʿĪsā, and forbidden to go home.

A letter from prison in Egypt, to his mother in Damascus
بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ

From Aḥmad ibn Taymiyya to my dear and honourable mother, may Allāh bless her amply, and grant her peace and comfort, and make her among the best of His servants.

It will not escape you, my contented mother, that our stay in Egypt is for a matter of weight, and to abandon it would be a corruption of our religion and of our life. Yet it was not our choice to be far from you. Had birds been able to carry us, we would have come to you. But the absent one has his reason, and had you been able to look deeply into the affairs of the Muslims, you would not choose for me any place other than the one I am in now.

Allāh has opened for me gates of blessing and mercy and guidance in a way I had never conceived. And still I am always considering the journey towards you, and making istikhāra. It is inconceivable to me, if I were given the choice, that I would prefer any of this life's business, or any lesser duty of the religion, over being near you.

I beseech you to supplicate for us abundantly. Ask Him to guide us to the best of our paths, for He knows and we do not, and He is able and we are weak.

Convey my salām to the whole household, young and old, and to the neighbours and the friends and the relatives, one by one.

Preserved in al-ʿUqūd al-durriyya · abridged
٧٠٨ هـ · 708 AH · 1309 CE · seven months

A tower by the sea

The new sultan belonged to the Sufi circle he had opposed. He was arrested, carried down to Alexandria and shut in a tower of the sultan's palace for seven months.

There, with the sea outside the wall, he wrote Kitāb al-Radd ʿalā al-manṭiqiyyīn, described in our own time as one of the most devastating attacks ever levelled at the logical system of the Greeks and their Muslim followers. It is the pattern of his whole life. Take away his freedom and he produces a book. Take away his books and he produces them out of his head.

In the prisons of Egypt

The prison that became a madrasa

He came in and found the prisoners at chess and dice, missing their prayers. He rebuked them hard, put them back on the prayer, turned them towards repentance and good work, and taught them from the Sunna what they needed.

The place turned into a haven for anyone who wanted to learn, better, they said, than the schools and the circles outside. Some men, when their sentences ended, chose to stay with him rather than walk out. Others came in from the city to sit with him, until the prison was full of them.

In the sultan's court, Cairo

Al-Nāṣir Muḥammad, restored to the throne, stood up for him in front of the princes and took him aside. Some of the scholars in this room, he said, swore allegiance to my rival and slandered you, and he asked for a fatwā to have them killed.

If they were to go, you would not find their like in your country again. As for what they did to me, and my right to take it out of them. I forgive them. They are free.

And earlier, when the same sultan accused him of wanting power, he said, I said that? By Allāh, your kingdom and the kingdom of the Mongols are worth nothing to me. The sultan laughed and said, by Allāh, you are a truthful man, and the one who told me this about you is a liar.

٧١٢ هـ · 712 AH · 1313 CE

Home, and a boy named Ibn al-Qayyim

He came back to Damascus with the army. A new governor raised him to the rank of professor, and his followers now held him an independent mujtahid, bound to no living authority.

And in these years he began teaching the student who would carry him further than any book of his own, Shams al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Abī Bakr, whom the world knows as Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya. Beside him were al-Mizzī, al-Dhahabī, Ibn ʿAbd al-Hādī, Ibn Kathīr, Ibn Muflih, Ibn Qāḍī al-Jabal, and ʿUmar al-Bazzār, who wrote down everything he saw.

It is most likely in this window, 713 to 717, that he wrote Darʾ taʿāruḍ al-ʿaql wa-l-naql, ten volumes on whether reason and revelation can ever truly collide. Peace lasted four years. In 716 the old quarrel between Ḥanbalīs and Ashʿarīs in Damascus broke into the open again.

٧١٨-٧٢١ هـ · 718-721 AH

He would not stop giving fatwā

He held that a triple divorce uttered in a single sitting counts as one repudiation, not three, and therefore does not make the divorce irrevocable if the man did not intend it. Ordinary men and their households turned on this. The four schools, his own among them, held otherwise.

The sultan ordered him to stop issuing fatwā on divorce contrary to the Ḥanbalī doctrine. A council in 718 examined the matter, then another in 719. He was cleared both times. A third, in 720, did not charge him with error at all. It charged him with insubordination for continuing to answer people who asked. For that he was arrested and put in the citadel of Damascus for five months.

Every person requires proof for what he says, and his saying is not itself a proof, except Allāh and His Messenger ﷺ.

٧٢٦ هـ · 726 AH · 1326 CE · two years

The last cell

The charge was a treatise, al-Risāla fī ziyārat al-qubūr, on visiting graves and seeking aid from the buried. He was arrested for the sixth time, stripped of the right to issue any fatwā at all, and returned to the citadel of Damascus. He stayed two full years.

He kept writing inside it. Among what came out of that cell was a reply to al-Ikhnāʾī, naming him. The judge complained to the sultan.

Entering the cell, he was heard reciting, and a wall will be raised between them, with a gate in it. Inside it mercy, and outside it torment.

Five months before the end

They took away the paper,
the ink, and the pens.

By order of the sultan, on a complaint from a judge. For a man who had written from memory in the dark of the Cairo citadel, this was the one wall he could not write his way out of. He turned to what was left him, the Qurʾān, from memory, again and again, and five months later he died.

His biographer writes that he passed away as if overwhelmed by grief at being denied the means to write.

20 Dhū al-Qaʿda 728 · 26 September 1328

He forgave them first

He was ill for a few days. Shams al-Dīn al-Wazīr was in Damascus and asked leave to visit him. He sat with him and apologised for whatever he had fallen short of in the shaykh's rights.

Then, in the night, he went on to the mercy of his Lord, in the morning, doing what he had always been doing, striving, patient, expecting his reward, and showing not the least cowardice or weakness or wavering. When he died he was occupied with Allāh and with nothing else.

The deputy of the prison came to give condolences and opened the door for those who loved him. They sat by him and wept. Then they washed him, al-Ḥāfiẓ al-Mizzī among them, and a company of the people of knowledge and faith.

I have forgiven him, and every one of my enemies who did not know that I was upon the truth.

I have forgiven King al-Nāṣir for imprisoning me. He did it on what he was told by others, not out of his own intent, and he is excused for it.

I have forgiven everyone who had a quarrel with me, except an enemy of Allāh and of His Messenger.
His last recorded words about the men who put him there
The janāzah · جنازته

Damascus stopped

The moment the news went round, nobody in Damascus who could come stayed away. Shops closed. Work stopped. Governors, scholars, jurists, soldiers, generals, men, women and children came out into the streets.

The crowd pressed in from every corner until it was feared the bier would go down before it reached the grave, and the authorities had to hold the people back with all their strength, which only made the crowd push harder. They prayed over him in the Umayyad Mosque, and it could not hold them. A great mass still stood outside.

He was carried on the hands of the people of Damascus and laid in the Maqbarat al-Ṣūfiyya, beside the grave of his brother ʿAbdullāh, a public cemetery of the city, named after a lodge that had once stood next to it. His brother ʿAbd al-Raḥmān prayed over him. Then the rest of the people prayed at the graveside, in turns, all day.

What remains

He left no dīnār and no dirham

What he left was his students, and they carried him. Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, who ordered and softened and spread everything his teacher had said. Al-Dhahabī, who wrote the history of the men of ḥadīth. Ibn Kathīr, whose tafsīr is in every house. Al-Mizzī. Ibn ʿAbd al-Hādī, who gathered his life into al-ʿUqūd al-durriyya. And ʿUmar al-Bazzār, who simply wrote down what he had watched a man do with his days.

Al-Bazzār said he never entered a country without finding one of his books in it. Seven centuries later that is still true, and the works written inside prison walls are still read by people who have never heard the name of the judge who put him there.

He was buried in the Maqbarat al-Ṣūfiyya, a public burial ground of Damascus that carried the name of a lodge built beside it long before, and nothing more than that. Centuries later the cemetery was cleared to make room for a hospital and almost all of it went. His grave was the one they left standing. It is there still, in an open yard among the buildings of Damascus University, three plain graves side by side with no dome over them and nothing built above the ground. The man who spent his last years arguing that no grave should be turned into a destination has exactly the grave he argued for.

The state of the Ummah

No caliph with power anywhere. A shadow ʿAbbāsid in Cairo who signed what the Mamlūks put in front of him. Slave-soldiers ruling Egypt and Shām, deposing each other every few years. A Mongol empire on the eastern border that had become Muslim without becoming safe.

How people lived

A Damascus of walled quarters and gates shut at night, with caravanserais, hammams, the Ghūṭa orchards feeding the city. Learning was free and public, so a porter could sit in the same circle as a judge. Famine and plague were normal. So was the sudden arrival of an army.

And in religion

Shrines and saints' tombs drew crowds. The monism of Ibn ʿArabī's followers was fashionable among the learned and the powerful. Kalām had become the language of the schools. He set himself against all three at once, which is why so much of his life was spent explaining himself to a tribunal.
